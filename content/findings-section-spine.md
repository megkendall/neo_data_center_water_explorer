## Struggle

Seven Northeast Ohio water-system cases were selected as analytical
comparators, ranging from Shalersville's 4 MGD documented peak
treatment capacity to Cleveland's 540 MGD documented treatment
capacity. Seven facility cases were selected specifically because they
had the strongest available public evidence. Even so, capacity evidence
existed for six of the seven and cooling evidence for all seven,
although some evidence was qualified. No complete facility-level
average or annual water-consumption figure, or facility-specific WUE,
was publicly established for any of them. The limited quantitative
water-use figures that were found were not equivalent to complete
facility consumption: Shalersville's 350 gpd figure was scope-limited,
while Perry's 273,000 gpd figure was a maximum allocation for all
facility water uses. Water-provider relationships fared no better: one
facility had a reported site-provider relationship, two had proposed or
uncertain relationships, and four had none publicly established.

## One day

With facility-level water data insufficient to estimate actual
consumption across the analytical set, a standardized scenario model
provided a way to explore potential implications without filling those
gaps with facility-specific estimates: hold standardized capacity and
water-intensity assumptions constant and ask what the same modeled
demand would mean in different documented water-system contexts.

## Because of that

Three parallel findings emerged:

### 1. Modeled water demand is not a simple function of megawatts

A 750 MW scenario modeled at the lowest evidence-informed intensity
(0.05 L/kWh) produces less modeled demand (0.24 MGD) than a 50 MW
scenario modeled at the highest intensity (1.50 L/kWh, 0.48 MGD) —
roughly half the modeled demand from fifteen times the capacity. At
fixed capacity, the highest-intensity scenario produces 30 times the
modeled demand of the lowest.

### 2. The same modeled demand carries very different relative weight depending on the comparison system

The highest standardized scenario (750 MW, 1.50 L/kWh) produces 7.13
MGD of modeled water demand. Using that identical modeled figure, it
represents 1.32% of Cleveland's documented treatment capacity and
178.3% of Shalersville's documented peak treatment capacity. These
percentages describe relative scale; they do not measure available
capacity, headroom, or whether a system could serve a project.

### 3. Cleveland's documented system-scale context complicates the idea of a single regional water story

Even the highest standardized scenario represents only 1.32% of its 540
MGD documented treatment capacity, a substantially different relative
scale from the smaller systems in the analytical set. This does not
establish that additional demand in Cleveland is inconsequential — only
that it is small relative to this particular documented system metric.

## Until finally

None of these figures describe how much water any of these seven
facilities will actually use, and none establish whether any of these
water systems has spare capacity to accommodate additional demand. What
they establish is that "Northeast Ohio is water-rich" is not, by
itself, an answer to a siting question. Modeled water demand depends
heavily on assumptions about water intensity, while the same
standardized modeled demand can mean something very different across
water systems. Evaluating a real proposal therefore requires
project-specific water-use and water-provider information that, across
these seven analytical cases, was often not publicly established in the
sources reviewed.
